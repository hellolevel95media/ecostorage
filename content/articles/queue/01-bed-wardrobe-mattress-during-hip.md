Bedroom furniture causes the most headaches during HIP. Beds and wardrobes are big, they usually sit close to the walls where the work happens, and a mattress is the one item nobody wants covered in fine grey dust. Because you will be sleeping on it again at the end of the works, it deserves more thought than any other piece in the room.

This guide covers how to decide what stays and what goes, how to clean and check fabric items before they are covered or stored, and how to wrap everything so it comes back in the condition it left.

## Why the bedroom is the hardest room

Dust from hacking and cement work is far finer than ordinary household dust. It settles on every surface and works its way into anything porous. Fabric, foam and unfinished wood are the worst affected, which describes almost everything in a bedroom. Mattresses, pillows, bedding, upholstered headboards and clothes all hold dust deep inside, and once it is in, it is difficult to remove.

Size is the other problem. A wardrobe or a bed frame cannot be tucked into a corner, so it either has to be protected in place or moved out of the unit. Deciding which is the first job.

## Step one: sort the room into three groups

Walk through the bedroom with a notepad and put each large item into one of three groups:

- Move out: mattresses, bedding, pillows, clothes, upholstered pieces and anything fabric
- Cover in place: solid wardrobes, built-in carpentry and heavy pieces that are not worth moving
- Dismantle and store: knock-down beds, shelving, desks and smaller furniture you can spare for a few weeks

A useful test is to ask whether the item can be properly sealed where it stands. A tall wardrobe against the wall being hacked cannot be fully protected, however good your sheeting is. A bed in the middle of the room, with the wall work on the far side, often can.

## Mattresses: check, clean, then protect

Mattresses are light enough to move and expensive to replace, so they are the item most worth protecting well. If you can move yours out of the unit, do it. If you cannot, treat it carefully.

Before you wrap or store a mattress, check it:

- Is it completely dry? Any dampness gets trapped under a cover and can turn into mould within days in Singapore's humidity
- Are there stains, odours or marks that will set if left for weeks?
- Are there signs of pests, such as small dark spots, shed skins or tiny eggs along seams and tags?
- Is there existing damage, such as a torn cover or sagging, that you should photograph first?

## A simple DIY mattress clean

A light clean before covering gives you a fresh mattress at the end of the works, and stops existing dust and skin cells being sealed in.

- Strip the bed and wash the bedding, mattress protector and pillowcases on a hot wash if the labels allow
- Vacuum the whole surface slowly with an upholstery attachment, including the sides, the seams and the underside if you can lift it
- Spot-clean marks using a cloth dampened with a little mild detergent in cold water. Blot, do not rub, and do not soak the mattress, particularly if it is foam
- Sprinkle a thin layer of baking soda over the surface, leave it for several hours or overnight to absorb odours, then vacuum it up thoroughly
- Let the mattress air for a few hours in a well-ventilated room, away from direct sun for long periods, which can fade the fabric

Make sure the mattress is completely dry before it goes under any cover. If you are unsure, give it another day.

## Covering a mattress you are leaving in the unit

Plastic sheeting looks protective, but sealed tightly it can trap moisture. For a mattress that stays in the flat for weeks, a better approach is to use a zipped mattress bag or a breathable cover first, then a loose outer layer of plastic to catch dust, taped to itself rather than pressed hard against the fabric. Keep the mattress away from the wall being worked on, and raise it slightly off the floor if you can, for example on its frame or on a clean pallet or board.

## Wardrobes: empty them first

A wardrobe that stays in the unit should be completely empty. Clothes left inside absorb dust through the door gaps, however well the outside is wrapped.

Empty it, then wipe the inside with a slightly damp cloth, and leave the doors open for an hour to dry. Wrap the outside in sheeting and tape it to the floor and ceiling edges so dust cannot creep in from above or below. Tape the doors shut with painter's tape, which is gentle on finishes. If there are mirrors or glass panels, put cardboard over them to protect against knocks.

Clothes you take out need their own care. Wash and fully dry them before packing, because damp fabric in a closed box is a mould risk. Pack them in breathable cartons or wardrobe boxes rather than airtight bags. Moisture absorbers such as silica gel packets help in a humid climate.

## Bed frames, headboards and smaller pieces

Knock-down frames can usually be taken apart, wrapped in sections and stored flat. This frees up floor space that the contractors will appreciate, and it protects the finish from knocks. As you dismantle:

- Photograph the frame before and during dismantling
- Put screws, bolts and fittings in a labelled bag and tape it to the largest piece
- Wrap slats and rails together with stretch film
- Use cardboard or foam on corners and edges

Upholstered headboards act like a mattress for dust, so move them out if you can. Wooden bedside tables and desks can be emptied, wiped and wrapped like a wardrobe.

## Photograph everything

Whatever you decide, take dated photos of each piece before it is wrapped or moved, including any existing scratches or marks. If something is damaged during works, a dated photo makes any conversation with the contractor far easier. Take a photo of the room layout too, so you can put it back the way it was.

## After HIP: unwrap and air

When the works are complete, do not unwrap furniture in the middle of a freshly cleaned room. Take items outside or to a service area, remove the sheeting gently so settled dust does not drift, wipe surfaces and vacuum fabric. Let the mattress air for a few hours before you make the bed. Dust that stuck to the outside of the wrapping stays on the wrapping, which is exactly what it is there for.

## When it is easier to move things out entirely

Protecting bulky furniture in place takes time and materials, and the room still feels cramped during the works.
