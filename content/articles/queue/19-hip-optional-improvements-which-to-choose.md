Of everything in the HIP process, the optional improvements are the part where your own decisions count most. The essential works happen to every flat in the block, but the optional items are yours to accept or decline. Each choice affects what you pay, how long your flat is disrupted and how much you have to clear out of the way.

## What you can and cannot decline

The Essential Improvements are not negotiable. They cover the repairs and replacements that keep older blocks safe, such as concrete repairs and new waste stacks, and the Government funds them for Singapore Citizen households.

The Optional Improvements are different. You can opt out of all of them and pay nothing for them. One limit applies to the toilet: you can only decline toilet upgrading if your toilets pass a water test for leaks. A toilet that fails the test needs the work whether you want it or not.

Senior-friendly fittings under the EASE programme sit alongside the package and are chosen separately. The Government pays the larger share of the cost for Singapore Citizen households.

## Questions to ask about each item

Rather than going down the list and ticking boxes, put every item through the same four questions:

- Will my household use this every day? Items you notice daily earn their cost faster.
- What condition is the current fitting in? A door or fitting in good shape rarely needs replacing.
- How much extra work does this create in my flat? More items mean more of your flat is affected, and that means more to protect.
- Does the co-payment fit my budget? The share is a percentage of the cost, so it rises with the number of items taken.

A fitting that is already in good condition and a toilet that passes its water test are strong reasons to decline.

## How your choices change the amount of disruption

HDB's guidance puts the work in each unit at about 10 working days, though that depends on the items selected and the condition of the flat. An owner who takes the full optional package has more of the flat opened up than one who takes only the essentials.

The toilet is usually the biggest factor. Upgrading it means hacking floors and walls, which produces the most dust and noise and puts the toilet out of action for part of the works. If you take that item, plan how the household will manage without it for the affected days, and keep a clear route through the flat for the workers.

## Matching your choices to your preparation

Each item you choose points to something you should prepare:

- Toilet upgrading: clear the bathroom completely, take down mirrors and shelves and seal the doorway against dust.
- Door and gate replacement: clear the entrance, move shoe cabinets and keep the walkway free.
- Electrical or wall work: unplug and pack electronics, cover walls and move furniture away from them.
- Senior-friendly fittings: confirm placement with the household member who will use them.

Working out this list early gives you time to arrange help, whether that is family, a cleaner or a team to move furniture.

## What to move out first

Whatever you choose, some items are better out of the flat for the duration:

- Mattresses and upholstered furniture, which absorb dust and are costly to clean.
- Electronics, particularly televisions and computers.
- Wardrobes and cabinets near work areas, which are heavy and hard to protect in place.
- Anything fragile or valuable that you would rather not worry about.

Plastic sheeting helps with items that stay, but dust finds its way through gaps in even careful wrapping. For the most vulnerable items, removing them from the flat is a simpler, more reliable approach.

## Ask people who have been through it

Neighbours in blocks that finished HIP earlier are the best source of practical advice. Ask which items they were glad to take, which ones they would skip and how long the toilet was actually out of use. Residents groups and block chat groups often hold this knowledge already, and an hour of conversation can save you from a choice you regret. Then compare what you hear with the details in your HDB letter, since schedules and fittings vary between precincts.

## A short decision routine

If the list feels long, a short routine cuts it down:

- Read your HDB letter and mark the deadline for choosing.
- Test your toilet for leaks, or review the result of the water test.
- Walk through the flat and note which fittings are worn out.
- Choose only the items you would pay to replace yourself.
- Plan what to clear for each item you take.

Households that follow this order tend to feel in control of the process. They know what is happening in their flat and why, and they have time to prepare for it.
